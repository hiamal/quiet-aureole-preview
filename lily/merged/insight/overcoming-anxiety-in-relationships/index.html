Overcoming Anxiety in Relationships

Lily’s blogFebruary 01, 20262 min read

When Relationships Reveal Patterns We Didn't Know We Were Carrying

Relationships don't always create our triggers.

Sometimes, they reveal them.

When anxiety appears in a relationship, it is easy to focus on the other person:

What are they doing?

Why aren't they listening?

Why won't they change?

Sometimes those questions are completely valid.

But another question can be just as revealing:

What is happening within me?

And:

What am I hoping to get from this?

Are you expressing what you genuinely need, or trying to make the other person behave differently so you can feel safe, accepted or in control?

When the Pattern Keeps Repeating

Relationship conflict can become a familiar cycle.

One person reacts.

The other responds.

The intensity builds.

Someone withdraws, explodes or shuts down.

Eventually, the conflict ends — but nothing has really changed.

Until the next time.

When we are inside the pattern, it can feel as though the other person is the problem.

But what if you became curious instead?

What am I protecting?

What am I afraid might happen?

What am I abandoning within myself to maintain this relationship?

Self-abandonment can look like saying yes when you mean no, suppressing your feelings to keep the peace, changing yourself to remain acceptable, or taking responsibility for someone else's emotions.

Over time, these responses can become so familiar that we stop recognising them as choices.

What Did You Learn About Love?

Our earlier experiences can influence what feels familiar, what we tolerate and what we perceive as normal.

If you learned that love meant pleasing, earning, managing or enduring, you may carry those patterns into relationships without realising it.

This does not make you responsible for another person's harmful or abusive behaviour. Responsibility for that behaviour belongs to the person choosing it.

But becoming curious about your own patterns can reveal why certain situations create such a strong response.

Sometimes, the experience has ended but the learning remains.

I describe this as an incomplete cycle.

Become the Observer

When anxiety or conflict arises, pause.

Notice what is happening within you and ask:

“Is this response coming from who I am now, or from something I learned I needed to do in order to feel safe, accepted or loved?”

That question creates space.

And within that space, choice becomes possible.

You may choose to communicate, set a boundary, compromise, stay or leave.

The difference is that the decision is no longer being made entirely by an unconscious pattern.

If you recognise yourself here, perhaps there is more beneath the reaction than you have yet understood.

A supportive therapeutic space can help you become curious about your own experience and what it may be trying to show you.

You don't have to keep repeating a pattern simply because it is familiar.
