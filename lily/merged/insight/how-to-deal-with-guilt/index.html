How to Deal With Guilt

Lily’s blogJune 07, 20262 min read

Understanding the Voice Behind the Feeling

Guilt is not always a sign that you have done something wrong.

Sometimes guilt reflects our values. We recognise that something we did wasn't right, take responsibility and make amends.

But sometimes guilt comes from somewhere else.

A rule we learned.

An expectation we absorbed.

A voice from a parent, teacher, caregiver or culture that became so familiar that we stopped questioning whether it was actually ours.

So when guilt appears, it can be worth asking:

Is this my belief — or a belief I learned?

When guilt becomes a way of living

For some people, guilt becomes almost a background state.

They feel guilty for resting.

For saying no.

For putting themselves first.

For needing help.

For disappointing someone.

For not being able to manage everything.

Over time, guilt can become so familiar that it feels like part of who we are.

One client I worked with was a husband and father who experienced recurring periods of depression, along with physical symptoms including tension, gut upset and pain.

As we explored what was happening beneath the surface, a strong belief emerged:

“I should be able to manage everything.”

He believed he should be strong, dependable and capable of carrying the responsibility for everyone around him.

And perhaps most importantly:

He shouldn't need help.

On the surface, these can sound like admirable qualities.

But what happens when being strong means there is no space to acknowledge that you are struggling?

What is guilt trying to tell you?

Sometimes guilt is pointing towards something that genuinely needs to be addressed.

But sometimes it is an old rule that no longer fits the person you have become.

The question becomes:

What am I actually responsible for?

And:

What am I carrying that may not be mine?

This is where becoming the observer can be powerful.

Rather than automatically obeying the feeling, you can become curious about it.

Where did I learn this?

Does this belief still align with my values?

What am I afraid would happen if I didn't feel guilty?

What would I choose if I wasn't trying to avoid disappointing someone?

You may discover that the guilt is asking you to repair something.

Or you may discover something very different:

“This isn't mine to carry anymore.”

That isn't irresponsibility.

That is discernment.

Guilt is not always a command.

Sometimes, it is an old voice.

And you get to decide whether it still belongs in your life.

If guilt, self-blame or the feeling that you should manage everything is affecting how you live or relate to others, exploring what sits beneath that feeling can create a different perspective.
