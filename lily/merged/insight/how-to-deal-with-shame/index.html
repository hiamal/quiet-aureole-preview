How to Deal With Shame

Lily’s blogJuly 05, 20262 min read

Understanding the Difference Between Shame and Guilt

Shame can feel similar to guilt, but there is an important difference.

Guilt says: “I did something wrong.”

Shame says: “There is something wrong with me.”

Guilt can sometimes help us recognise when our actions have moved away from our values. Shame can take an experience and turn it into an identity.

“I made a mistake” becomes “I'm a failure.”

“I couldn't cope” becomes “I'm weak.”

“Someone treated me badly” becomes “There must be something wrong with me.”

And shame can often hide inside the word “should.”

“I should have known better.”

“I should have been stronger.”

“I should be able to cope.”

But who decided?

Where did that rule come from?

And does it still belong to you?

When an Experience Becomes a Belief

Our experiences teach us how to navigate the world. We adapt, protect ourselves and find ways to keep going.

Sometimes those adaptations serve us well.

But sometimes, what helped us cope becomes a belief about who we are.

“I'm not enough.”

“I'm weak.”

“I'm not in control.”

Over time, these beliefs can become so familiar that we stop questioning them.

This is where becoming the observer can be powerful.

Rather than being completely inside the story, we can begin to step back and become curious about what happened, what we learned from it and what we made it mean about ourselves.

What If Nothing Was Wrong With You?

One client I worked with had become exceptionally good at coping. They managed their family, business and an overwhelming personal situation while rarely asking anyone for help.

Eventually, their ability to keep managing reached its limit.

As we explored what lay beneath the surface, earlier experiences began to make sense in a different way.

What they had seen as weaknesses could instead be understood as adaptations.

They had found ways to cope.

To protect themselves.

To keep functioning.

The question changed from:

“What's wrong with me?”

to:

“What was I doing that made sense at the time?”

That is a very different question.

Because what protected you then may not be what you need now.

You can begin to separate:

What happened to me

from

who I am.

You are not the experience.

You are not the reaction you developed to it.

And you don't have to remain defined by something simply because it shaped you.

If something in this article resonates with the way you see yourself, perhaps there is a story beneath the shame that is asking to be understood.

You don't have to work that out alone.

A supportive, impartial space can help you become curious about what is beneath the surface, without judgement and without requiring you to relive what happened.
